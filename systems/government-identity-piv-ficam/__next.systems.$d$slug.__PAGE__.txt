## What this system is

Government identity is the **identity backbone under every federal control family (IA/AC)**: employees and contractors prove who they are with a **PIV card** (Personal Identity Verification, FIPS 201) or, for the Department of Defense, a **CAC** — a smart card holding a certificate whose private key never leaves the card. The **FICAM** (Federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management) architecture is how those credentials federate across agencies, and **NIST SP 800-63** is the ladder that says *how much* assurance a citizen-facing transaction needs.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    U[User - employee, contractor, citizen] --> C[Credential - PIV / CAC / account]
    C --> P[Identity provider<br/>FICAM trusted flow]
    P --> F[Federation - SAML / OIDC]
    F --> A[Federal applications]
    A --> P
```

## The three reassurance levels (NIST 800-63)

SP 800-63 separates *how sure you are* into three axes. Getting these backwards is the classic federal identity mistake:

| Level | Axis | What it asks | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **IAL** | Identity assurance | How strongly was the *person* proven to exist? | (L1) self-asserted, (L2) docs, (L3) biometric-in-person |
| **AAL** | Authenticator assurance | How strongly are they authenticated *now*? | (L1) password, (L2) PLUS a second factor, (L3) hardware crypto |
| **FAL** | Federation assurance | How much trust does the federation pass along? | (L1) minimal, (L2) signed assertions, (L3) identity binding |

The rule that animates everything: **a transaction's risk sets the required level.** Renewing a library card online is IAL1/AAL1; a veteran reading their own medical record or a small business accessing benefits is IAL2/AAL2; a classified-grade authenticator is AAL3 hardware. When someone says "you need 800-63 up-leveled authentication", they mean push AAL, sometimes IAL — and each step costs real user friction and infrastructure.

## The credential lifecycle (the PIV card)

```mermaid
flowchart TB
    R[Identity proofing - in person] --> a{Sponsored<br/>by agency}
    a --> C[Issue - PIV/CAC provisioned]
    C --> U[Use - authentication at every door]
    U --> M[Maintain - renew, update certs]
    M --> L[Revoke - lost, departed, CA fired]
    L --> D[Terminate - card destroyed, access killed]
```

## The architecture pattern

```mermaid
sequenceDiagram
    participant U as Employee / contractor - PIV or CAC
    participant I as Identity proofing - FIPS 201 enrollment
    participant CA as Certificate authority - issues PIV certs
    participant P as FICAM identity provider
    participant F as Federation - SAML / OIDC trust chain
    participant A as Agency application
    U->>I: prove identity
    I->>CA: enroll
    CA-->>U: card issued
    U->>P: authenticate
    P->>F: assert identity
    F->>A: SSO session
```

Every hop exists to preserve one property: **proofing happens once, at high assurance; authentication happens everywhere, cheaply, via a federation of trust** — the same principle as [Enterprise SSO](../single-sign-on-sso) applied to multiple independent agencies.

## The pro se / citizen side (eID and login.gov-style)

For citizens there is no employer-issued card, so the trust anchors are different: a **federated login broker** (USAGov login / login.gov) verifies the citizen once at IAL2 (document checks now, biometric where risk demands) and issues a reusable authenticator that agencies accept via federation. The key concepts there:

- **Federation broker, not a database of everything**: it asserts identity + assurance level; each agency still applies its own authorization on top ([RBAC, policy, least privilege](../rbac-and-authorization)).
- **AAL can float**: an agency can accept AAL1 for read-only and demand AAL2 before a write action — keep login cheaper on low-risk reads.
- **Consent & transparency**: the citizen sees exactly what attribute is being shared — government identity is also a [verifiable-credential story](../decentralized-identity-and-verifiable-credentials) in another hat.

## When this shows up in architecture

- **Access control (AC/IA) controls in an ATO**: RMF's IA family basically assumes PIV/CAC for employees and 800-63 levels for citizens — [FedRAMP](../fedramp-nist-rmf) assessments check how cleanly you inherit and consume that.
- **Agency shared services**: FICAM says agencies should consume one shared IdP rather than each building its own credential store.
- **Contractor onboarding**: the same PIV chain covers employees *and* contractors — which for security is why offboarding ([joiner-mover-leaver](../joiner-mover-leaver)) is a compliance event, not just an HR one.

## Tester checklist

- PIV/CAC auth: a PIV card logs in without the private key ever leaving the card (verify by checking no client key extraction is even possible).
- Assurance float: the same citizen is authenticated at AAL1 for read and stepped up to AAL2 before a write/action, and the app honors the declared AAL.
- Federation break: a revoked PIV/citizen account is denied at the application within the revocation SLA across every federated partner.
- Zero proofing reuse: a citizen ID verified at a broker has no hidden path to impersonate a separate real-world identity without going through IAL2 again.
- Evidence of level: audit records show the IAL/AAL/FAL used per transaction so a 800-63 assertion is retraceable to a dated decision.
- Cross-agency SSO: one PIV login reaches multiple agency apps through the federation chain with no re-password prompts.
