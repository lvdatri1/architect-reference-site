## What this system is

Change enablement is the governance layer that lets an organisation **modify a service without breaking it**. It decides *who may authorise what*, when, and with how much ceremony, then release and deployment management carries the approved change into production. The question every change answers: *what risk do we absorb, and who signs for it?*

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  R[Change request] --> A[Assess - impact + risk]
  A --> C[CAB or pre-authorised]
  C --> W[Dispatch - schedule + backout]
  W --> B[Build + test]
  B --> D[Deploy to production]
  D --> P[Post-implementation review]
```

A change is **not** the same as a release. The change is the *decision and the plan* (what changes, with what risk, on whose authority); the release is the *assembly* (a build that bundles one or more approved changes); the deployment is the *act* of putting that release into a live environment. Governance lives on the change; mechanics live on the release.

## Change types: three paths, three weights

| Type | Authority | Ceremony | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Standard** | Pre-approved (in the catalogue) | None per change — it's already authorised | Password resets, adding a user to a group, routine patching runs |
| **Normal** | Change Advisory Board (CAB) | Assess, approve, schedule, review | Service or infra changes with real blast radius |
| **Emergency** | Emergency CAB (ECAB), or after-the-fact if time-critical | Compressed or retroactive | Fixing a P1 outage, critical security patch, urgent data recovery |

Standard changes are the design goal: **if a change is routine, you shouldn't need a meeting every time** — pre-authorise once and let it flow. Normal changes are where the risk conversation happens. Emergency changes exist to be broken by design; the discipline is the mandatory post-implementation review, where an emergency that becomes a habit is diagnosed.

## The change lifecycle

1. **Request** — a ticket with description, rationale, owner, and target environment. Vague requests get bounced before the CAB ever sees them.
2. **Assess** — impact and probability, what could break, and — critically — the **backout plan**: how you get back to the previous state.
3. **Authorise** — the CAB (or the pre-authorised standard path) signs off with conditions. The keyword in the minutes is always "approved **with** the named mitigations".
4. **Dispatch** — schedule into a change window with a start, an end, and an **owner of the window**; late overruns are failures.
5. **Build + test** — the release is assembled (via [CI/CD pipelines](../ci-cd-pipelines)) and verified against the plan.
6. **Deploy** — the change leaves the CAB's hands; the deployment team executes with freeze discipline.
7. **Review** — post-implementation review confirms what actually happened, what deviated, and whether the change page learned anything for the next one.

## The CAB: who signs for risk

A Change Advisory Board is **not a rubber stamp and not a bottleneck** — it's the calibrated, delegated decision point. It works best when it's small and relevant: a service owner, the team whose environment is changing, an ops or infra lead, a security or architecture rep when the risk says so, and the change manager as facilitator. Its output is a decision the record can point to: date, attendees, conditions, and the named accountable owner. Emergencies get the ECAB — or documented after-the-fact authorisation when the window cannot wait.

```mermaid
stateDiagram-v2
  [*] --> Request
  Request: Change request
  Request --> Assess: ticket
  Assess: Assess risk + backout
  Assess --> CAB: change record
  CAB: CAB / pre-authorised
  CAB --> Build: approved - build
  CAB --> Deploy: approved - load
  Build: Build + test release
  Build --> Deploy: release ready
  Deploy: Deploy in change window
  Deploy --> Review: verify + learn
  Review: Post-implementation review
  Review --> [*]
```

## The service catalog and service requests

**Service catalog** is the menu of what IT actually sells — the user-visible catalog (email, VPN, CRM) mapped to the technical catalog that delivers it (Exchange, gateway, the CRM app). Every catalog item should state its price in business terms: *availability, capacity, continuity, security*.

**Service requests** (a new laptop, an account unlock, a software install) are the **standard-change** pathway made visible to users — pre-authorised, no CAB, fulfilled against SLAs. The catalog and the request catalogue are the same lever: what you're willing to *promise* and what you're willing to *do quickly without a meeting*.

## SLAs, OLAs, and UCs — making promises real

The service-level stack is the contract ladder between "what we promise" and "who actually has to deliver it":

| Layer | Between | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SLA** | Service provider and customer | 99.9% availability; P1 response in 15 minutes |
| **OLA** | Two internal teams | Networking resolves tickets within 4 hours of escalation |
| **UC** | Provider and external supplier | Vendor patches the firewall within 3 business days |

Tracing this ladder is how "the SA promised 99.9%" becomes a real, auditable, assigned obligation — and it's exactly where most SLA brokenness hides: the OLA and UC limbs of the chain were never written down.

## Tester checklist

- Take an approved normal change and confirm the backout plan is concrete: who to call, what command, how long to revert, and a test for "we are back".
- Verify the standard-change catalogue actually covers the routine things people do, and that a standard change never needs a CAB meeting.
- Drill an emergency change: trigger it during the outage, confirm the retro-authorisation got recorded and a post-implementation review is mandatory, not optional.
- Audit change windows: does the deployment record show the window's real start and end, and who was accountable for the overrun?
- Walk an SLA to its UC: pick one public promise and confirm an OLA + UC exist that would actually be breached first.
