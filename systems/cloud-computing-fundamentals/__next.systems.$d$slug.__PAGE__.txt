## What this system is

The cloud is a managed utility: you rent compute, storage, and network at *region* scale instead of owning data centers. The core mental model — **regions + availability zones (AZs) + service models** — is identical across AWS and Azure even when the names differ.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  App[Your app<br/>deployed across AZs] -- compute --> AZA[Availability zone A]
  App -- compute --> AZB[Availability zone B]
  AZA -- primary writes --> DB1[(Managed DB - primary)]
  AZB -- replica reads --> DB2[(Managed DB - standby)]
  DB2 -- failover --> DB1
  DB1 -- "RPO/RTO met" --> OK[Keeps serving<br/>one zone fails]
```

```mermaid
flowchart TB
  subgraph Region["Region: us-east-1 / East US"]
    direction LR
    AZ1[Availability zone A<br/>separate building]
    AZ2[Availability zone B<br/>separate building]
    AZ3[Availability zone C<br/>separate building]
  end
  App[Your app - deployed across 2+ AZs] --> AZ1
  App --> AZ2
```

A **region** is a geographic cluster of 2-4+ **availability zones** — independent power, cooling, and fibre. A true cloud deployment tolerates losing one whole AZ and keeps serving. That is the single biggest resilience idea in cloud.

## The three service models

| | IaaS | PaaS | SaaS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| You manage | App + some config | App | Nothing but usage |
| AWS | EC2, VPC | RDS, Lambda, ECS | S3? no — SaaS is like Office 365 |
| Azure | VMs, VNet | App Service, Functions | SaaS SKUs you consume |
| Best for | Legacy lift, full control | Ship apps fast, minimal ops | Just-use it |

Your job as a Cloud Engineer is mostly **choosing the model per workload**: databases on IaaS means patching MySQL yourself; on PaaS you get patching, backups, and failover for free — and pay accordingly.

## Shared responsibility model

```mermaid
flowchart TB
  subgraph Provider["Cloud provider (AWS / Azure)"]
    A[Physical security, hardware, network]
    B[Hypervisor, host patching, managed services]
  end
  subgraph You["Customer"]
    C[Your data, access policies, applications]
    D[Runtime config on IaaS, IAM, encryption keys]
  end
```

The boundary moves depending on the service: with **IaaS** you own OS-to-top; with **PaaS/SaaS** the provider owns almost everything below your code — but *you always* own your data, your IAM, and your key material. "The cloud encrypted it" is never a substitute for your own encryption decisions.

## Designing for failure

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  L1[Load balancer across AZs] --> V1[App in AZ A]
  L1 --> V2[App in AZ B]
  DB[(Managed DB<br/>multi-AZ replica)]
  DB -->|failover| DB2[(Standby AZ C)]
  B2[(Backup policy<br/>RPO/RTO defined)]
```

Three numbers shape every design: **RPO** (how much data you can lose) and **RTO** (how fast you must be back) and **cost** (what you'll pay for both). Multi-AZ + automated failover shrinks RPO/RTO; backups are the safety net when both fail.

## Cost levers (the art of not overspending)

- **Right-size then autoscale**: most cloud bill statements are runaway idle VMs.
- **Reserved/commitment plans** for steady load, **spot** for batch/fault-tolerant work.
- **Storage tiers**: hot/cold/archive — put the audit logs in archive, not hot S3/Blob.
- **Serverless** (Lambda/Functions) is often the cheapest for spiky workloads and the most expensive for sustained steady load.

## Tester checklist

- Kill/stop one AZ's instances (in a staging account) and verify the app re-serves from the remaining AZs within your RTO.
- Restore from a backup into a scratch region and confirm RPO is at most your stated window.
- Verify IAM/RBAC is least-privilege: no cloud-admin on daily users, no giant ALLOW on read buckets.
- Watch a month of cost report: which two line items most exceed the forecast? Right-size them.
