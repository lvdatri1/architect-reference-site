## What this system is

A bank's own ledger is local truth — but money moves between banks through **payment schemes**: shared rulebooks and message networks that let Bank A move value to Bank B on another ledger. The journey has three distinct phases, and confusing them is the classic error in payments architecture:

| Phase | What happens | Risk it bears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Initiation** | The customer orders a payment; the bank checks funds and sends a message | Rejects, AML, limits |
| **Clearing** | Schemes exchange and match messages, net positions between banks | Message loss, dual processing |
| **Settlement** | Final transfer of value between bank accounts at the central bank (or via nostro accounts) | Settlement risk, liquidity |

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    C[Customer / corporate] --> O[Sender bank]
    O --> CH[Clearing house - message exchange + netting]
    CH --> R1[Recipient bank - credit account]
    O --> S1[Settlement at central bank<br/>debit sender's reserve]
    CH --> S1
    S1 --> S2[Settlement at central bank<br/>credit recipient's reserve]
    R1 --> BW[Beneficiary paid]
```

## The message is the money

Payments run on *messages*, not on moving files of cash. The modern standard is **ISO 20022**: XML messages (`pacs.008` credit transfer, `pacs.002` status, `camt.05X` statements) that carry a real payment and its lifecycle status. Legacy systems used format-per-rail (SWIFT MT10x, SEPA files, domestic files); ISO 20022 unifies them, which is why the industry calls it the language of the future rails. For an architect the practical consequence is: **design your payment domain model around statuses and idempotency keys, not around one wire format.**

```mermaid
sequenceDiagram
    participant SENDER as "Sender bank"
    participant SCHEME as "Scheme"
    participant CLEAR as "Clearing house"
    participant CB as "Central bank"
    participant BEN as "Beneficiary"
    SENDER->>SENDER: authorize - funds check + AML
    SENDER->>SCHEME: dispatch pacs.008
    SCHEME->>CLEAR: clear
    CLEAR->>CB: net + settle
    CLEAR->>BEN: advise credit
```

## Netting, and why settlement risk is the real enemy

If every payment settled the instant it cleared, a day with 100,000 transfers between the same two banks would move that many individual balances. **Netting** collapses them: each bank nets its per-counterparty position and settles *one* number. This is what makes modern payments volume possible — but it concentrates risk into settlement time:

- **Herstatt / settlement risk**: Bank A pays out on the agreed value date and Bank B fails before paying back. The classical mitigation is **delivery versus payment** (settlement only when the backing transfer is guaranteed) — the same principle as atomic commit in a distributed system.
- **Liquidity**: to net, a bank must fund its account at the central bank; running out is the operational nightmare that real-time gross settlement (RTGS) was invented to support — each payment settles individually against central-bank reserves, immediate and final, at the cost of needing more liquidity.

## The main rails compared

| Rail | Peer | Finality | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **RTGS** (Fedwire, TARGET2) | High-value interbank | Real-time, irrevocable | Corporate wires, settlement of other rails |
| **ACH / SEPA** | Batch, low-value | Deferred (next day or two) | Salaries, direct debits, consumer transfers |
| **Instant / RTP** (FedNow, SEPA Instant, UPI) | Real-time, low-value, 24/7 | Immediate | Retail payments, the mobile-app payment |
| **Card schemes** (Visa/MC) | Authorize-then-capture | Clearing after capture | E-commerce and point-of-sale |
| **SWIFT / cross-border** | Correspondent messaging | Two- or three-day correspondent chain | International wires, securities settlement |

The architectural pattern repeats across every rail: **idempotency on the outbound message → status tracking on the inbound status → a reconciliation feed from the clearing house**. Build that pattern once, and adding a new rail is a connector change, not a rearchitecture.

## When this shape shows up

- **Payments hub / enterprise middleware** — one internal service owns outbound schemes, dedupes, and retries, so the core ledger never sends a payment twice.
- **Instant payments**: 24/7 availability, real-time screening, and *the messaging moves at human speed* — a customer aborts a transfer before it "settles" at the speed of thought, so UX and rails must separate *intent* from *irrevocable*.
- **ISO 20022 migration**: the dreaded legacy-to-standard cutover, where the funding challenge is mapping corporate references and address code lists, not the XML.

## Tester checklist

- Idempotency: double-send the same payment request with the same key — exactly one message leaves the bank.
- Status flow: drive a payment through every status (initiated, rejected, cleared, settled, failed, returned) and confirm the internal state march matches the scheme's.
- Rejection path: a customer whose account has insufficient funds is rejected *before* a message is dispatched, with a clean customer-facing status.
- Netting correctness: simulate a day of both-way pairs between two banks and confirm per-counterparty nets and the central-bank settlement sum to zero.
- Reconciliation: the scheme statement (`camt.05X`) ties to internal postings line-by-line, including fees and technical returns.
- Settlement risk: fund the liquidity run solo (one party unfunded) and confirm settlement stalls cleanly, not half-settles.
- Message integrity: one malformed reference or code list value is caught at schema validation and routed to exception handling, not silently dropped.
