## What this system is

"Integration" in the business sense is different from the [integration patterns](../integration-patterns) that connect systems. Here it answers the strategy question: **which pieces of the value chain does the company own?** **Vertical integration** grows by owning more stages of a single chain — a carmaker also smelting its own steel, or manufacturing its own batteries. **Horizontal integration** grows by owning more of the *same stage* — a carmaker buying other carmakers, or an airline buying competitors. Both are growth theses about where value (and margin) sits, not software.

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  subgraph Chain["The value chain - raw material to customer"]
    RAW[Raw materials]
    MK[Manufacture]
    DIST[Distribution / logistics]
    RTL[Retail / customer]
  end
  subgraph V["Vertical integration = own more of the chain"]
    RAW --> MK
    MK --> DIST
    DIST --> RTL
  end
  subgraph H["Horizontal integration = own more of one step"]
    C1[Competitor A]
    C2[Competitor B]
    C3[Competitor C]
  end
  V ~~~ H
```

## The value chain, as architecture sees it

| Stage | Example companies operating there | What consolidating here buys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw materials | Miners, growers, chemical suppliers | Price/cost certainty, supply security |
| Components & manufacturing | Assembly, fabrication, OEMs | Control of cost, quality, production timing |
| Distribution & logistics | Freight, warehousing, 3PLs | Delivery control, margin capture, speed |
| Retail & customer | Storefronts, e-commerce, marketplaces | Direct customer relationships, data, margin |

The architect's job is to ask "where does the value chain run through *our* systems, and which adjacent stages should it run through too?" — because every integration move lands on the enterprise estate as a new supply chain, a new ERP/SCM footprint, or a new sales/platform system to graft on.

## Vertical integration: backward and forward

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  subgraph Backward["Backward - upstream"]
    SUPP[Supplier<br/>- we acquire this]
  end
  subgraph Firm["The firm"]
    MK[Manufacture<br/>our existing stage]
  end
  subgraph Forward["Forward - downstream"]
    RTL[Retail channel<br/>- we acquire this]
  end
  SUPP --> MK
  MK --> RTL
```

- **Backward integration** buys the *upstream* stage — the inputs. Why: raw-material cost swings, supplier bottlenecks, quality control, or a supplier earning too much margin.
- **Forward integration** buys the *downstream* stage — distribution or the customer. Why: capture the retail margin, control the customer experience, own the data and the relationship.
- **Full vertical integration** owns stages both directions (a retailer that also makes its products, a manufacturer that also runs its own logistics and stores).

The decisive trade: vertical integration converts an **arms-length market transaction into an internal one**. That removes a negotiation and a margin, but it also removes *price signal* and *specialist competition* — the internal mine is never benchmarked against outside mines, and inefficiency quietly moves up the chain. The value-chain economist's view: you want to own the stage where the **profit pool** is deepest, and outsource the stages where everybody can do it.

## Horizontal integration: scale at one stage

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Airline A] --> Merge[One larger airline]
  B[Airline B] --> Merge
  C[Airline C] --> Merge
  Merge --> Scale[Bigger network, fewer rivals, pricing power]
```

Horizontal integration is the classic **M&A growth play**: buy a competitor (or merge) to gain scale *at your own stage*. The logic:

- **Economies of scale** — fixed costs (factories, fleets, R&D, a banking platform) spread over more volume, driving unit cost down.
- **Market power** — fewer competitors means greater pricing power; anti-trust regulators are precisely the check here.
- **Network effects** — a bigger network/service/base is worth more per user (delivery coverage, flight routes, banking customers).
- **Cost synergy** — overlapping functions (two HQs, two ERPs, two IT estates) collapse into one.

The horizontal failure mode is the amalgamation problem: merging two companies into one **platform** is brutally harder than merging two balance sheets. Two different ERPs, two customer systems, two IT towers must become one — and the "synergies" evaporate if integration of systems costs more and takes longer than the savings.

## Make vs buy: the decision that generates both

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  Q[Should we own this stage?] --> A{Core to our differentiation?}
  A -->|yes - strategic| OWN[Integrate - vertically or via capability build]
  A -->|no - commodity| OUT[Buy / outsource - keep it arms-length]
  A -->|uncertain| TEST[Enough volume + margin to justify?]
  TEST -->|yes| OWN
  TEST -->|no| OUT
```

| Consideration | Favors owning (integrate) | Favors buying (outsource) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where is the margin? | The deepest profit pool, consistent quality | A thin-margin commodity, many suppliers |
| Strategic importance | Core capability, differentiation, IP | Table stakes anyone can do |
| Dependency risk | Supplier can bottleneck you | You can always switch, benchmark, or 3-bid |
| Capital & focus | You have capital and attention to spare | Capital is better deployed at your own stage |
| Tech intensity | Proprietary advantage (manufacturing tech, data) | Rapidly changing niche owned by specialists |

## The enterprise estate angle

Whatever the strategy, integration lands as **integration work** your estate must absorb:

- **Backward integration** extends the [supply chain](../supply-chain-management): new suppliers feed new procurement flows, inventory moves up the chain, and your ERP's purchasing/master-data footprint grows.
- **Forward integration** extends the customer surface: new sales channels, a new [CRM](../salesforce-crm) or POS to onboard, order-to-cash flows to wire.
- **Horizontal integration** is the hardest systems case: **two estates become one** — two ERPs, two customer systems, two payrolls have to run as one. That is a migration program of its own (the classic "all synergies are on the balance sheet, all costs are in the join"), and the enterprise-architecture work that keeps both sides running through it is where deals typically win or lose.

The architect's honest summary for leadership: the *strategy* is the easy 10%. The 90% is proving that two organizations' systems, data models, and processes can actually be joined — and that's the [enterprise architecture](../business-process-modeling-and-management) conversation.

## The classic mistakes

- **Integrating for margin that isn't there** — buying the mine *because* the supplier's margin looked fat, then discovering the margin was the supplier's reward for competence the owner can't replicate (governed by the make-vs-buy test).
- **Confusing the two axes** — "horizontal integration for synergy" when the real upside was vertical position; or buying volume at a stage where scale buys nothing (costs are already mostly variable).
- **Ignoring the amalgamation cost** — the deal banks synergy, but two different ERPs, tax systems, and payrolls take years and enormous spend to unify; the synergy often lands as one massive program of [integration patterns](../integration-patterns) work, not a margin line.
- **Anti-trust blindness** — horizontal moves reach regulator scrutiny; vertical moves can too when they foreclose competitors (e.g., owning both the platform and the stores who rival your merchants).
- **Doing strategy without the estate** — signing a vertical acquisition, then discovering the company can't run a supplier relationship across three SCM systems; the strategy must be bought *with* the roadmap to absorb it.

## Tester checklist

- Given a strategy announcement, lay out the concrete estate impact: which new systems, which [ERP](../enterprise-resource-planning) scope, which new [processes](../business-process-modeling-and-management) — the strategy is only real where it shows up in the roadmap.
- Map the new value chain onto the existing [supply chain](../supply-chain-management) model: which stage is now internal, what data crosses the new boundary.
- For a horizontal deal: inventory the system estates on each side and find the overlapping capabilities (two ERPs, two CRMs) — those are the amalgamation work list.
- Verify the make-vs-buy decision holds for a real component: does the "own" case still hold once capital, tech intensity, and switching cost are priced in?
- Check the profit-pool logic: the stage you integrate must be where margin actually accumulates; test the assumption against the numbers, not the narrative.
