Value chain and Porter analysis

Porter's value chain — primary and support activities — as the map of where margin and differentiation actually come from, and how it guides outsourcing, capability, and technology decisions.

Enterprise ArchitectSolution ArchitectCloud EngineerTester#enterprise-architecture#strategy#business-process#industry

What this system is

Michael Porter's value chain is a way of seeing a company as a chain of activities, each of which either adds value the customer will pay for or doesn't. The whole is compared to competitors via Porter's five forces (rivalry, threat of entrants, buyer power, supplier power, substitute threat), and the strategy that follows is "win by doing a set of activities differently than rivals, not by doing the same activities marginally better." Where supply chain management is the operational chain of things, the value chain is the analytical chain of activities and margins.

The analytical habit: for every activity ask does this add value the customer pays for, or is it support? Activities that do neither are cost with no return — the candidates for outsourcing, automation, or removal.

The margin view

Rendering diagram…

The chain's whole point is margin: every activity adds cost, and only some add value the customer perceives. Two strategies flow from this:

  • Cost leadership — optimize the chain so your total cost beats rivals at the same price. Cost is decided across the whole chain, not one activity: a company with a slightly more expensive factory but dramatically cheaper logistics can still lead on cost.
  • Differentiation — make specific activities (design, service, brand, the platform's developer experience) better than rivals so customers pay a premium. Differentiation lives in specific activities, not "being good overall."

The architecture translation: when you map value to systems, cost-leadership estates are built to minimize operational cost (consolidate, standardize, outsource the commodity, automate support); differentiation estates invest heaviest in the differentiated activities' systems (a best-in-class e-commerce platform, a proprietary analytics capability), even if the surrounding support systems stay generic.

Five forces: what shapes the value you can keep

Rendering diagram…

Porter's companion idea: your ability to keep margin is set by the structure around the chain, not just your own activities. Strong supplier power (few suppliers, switching costs) squeezes your cost side; strong buyer power squeezes your price side; entrants and substitutes cap what you can charge. The five forces explain why two identical chains in different industries have very different margins — the airline chain is structurally thin, the chip chain structurally fat.

The value chain is the strategy layer: it says which activities we must be best at. The supply chain and the business processes are the operation layers that then implement those activities. When a value chain says "service is our differentiator," the estate responds with a service platform, not a cheaper factory system.

Outsourcing and make-vs-buy, by activity

Rendering diagram…

The value chain turns make-vs-buy from a hunch into a checklist: an activity is a candidate for outsourcing when it is (a) support rather than primary, (b) a commodity anyone can do (IT support, payroll, logistics for many firms), or (c) a support activity where an external specialist is better and cheaper. It is a candidate for in-house investment when it is core to differentiation — because outsourcing a differentiator outsources the reason customers choose you.

The architectural questions

  • Where is the profit pool in your chain? Map the estate's investment budget against the activities that earn margin — the mismatch is where technology spend is being wasted.
  • Which activities differentiate? Those systems get the best-in-class treatment; everything else can be commodity, cloud, or off-the-shelf.
  • What do you actually outsource? Every outsourced activity becomes an integration problem: data crosses a company boundary, so the integration patterns apply, and a contract (SLA, API, data share) replaces an internal handshake.
  • Is your support stack cheap? Cost-leadership firms keep support systems lean and standardized; gold-plating support activities is the quiet margin leak.

Tester checklist

  • Map a real business to the nine activities and score each: primary vs support, core vs commodity, value-added vs pure cost — the map should identify at least one outsourcing candidate and one must-own activity.
  • For an outsourcing candidate, enumerate the estate impact: which data crosses the boundary, what integration (patterns, SLAs, APIs) must be built to replace the internal handshake.
  • Verify the differentiator is funded: is the differentiated activity's system budget larger than a support activity's, or has gold-plating crept into the commodity side?
  • Price the chain: sum the activity costs and confirm the margin claim — an activity that costs more than the value it adds is a candidate to cut, automate, or outsource.
  • Stress the five forces: pick one force (e.g., supplier power) and identify what the estate must build (multi-source tooling, switching options) to reduce that pressure.
