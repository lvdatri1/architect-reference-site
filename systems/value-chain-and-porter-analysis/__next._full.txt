## What this system is

Michael Porter's **value chain** is a way of seeing a company as a chain of activities, each of which either *adds value* the customer will pay for or *doesn't*. The whole is compared to competitors via **Porter's five forces** (rivalry, threat of entrants, buyer power, supplier power, substitute threat), and the strategy that follows is "win by doing a set of activities *differently* than rivals, not by doing the same activities marginally better." Where [supply chain management](../supply-chain-management) is the operational chain of *things*, the value chain is the analytical chain of *activities and margins*.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  subgraph Primary["Primary activities"]
    IL[Inbound logistics] --> OP[Operations]
    OP --> OL[Outbound logistics]
    OL --> MS[Marketing & sales]
    MS --> SV[Service]
  end
  subgraph Support["Support activities"]
    FI[Firm infrastructure]
    HR[Human resources]
    TD[Technology development]
    PR[Procurement]
  end
```

## Primary vs support activities

| Activity | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inbound logistics** | Receiving, storing, handling inputs | Raw material receiving, inventory control |
| **Operations** | Transforming inputs into products | Assembly, fabrication, testing, a banking platform |
| **Outbound logistics** | Distributing the finished product | Warehousing, order delivery, fulfillment |
| **Marketing & sales** | Making customers want and buy | Brand, pricing, channels, selling |
| **Service** | Supporting the product after sale | Support desk, maintenance, returns |
| **Firm infrastructure** | General management, finance, legal | Executive leadership, planning, compliance |
| **Human resources** | Hiring, training, compensation | Recruiting, people systems |
| **Technology development** | Know-how and technology across the chain | R&D, IT systems, engineering |
| **Procurement** | Sourcing inputs (not the inputs themselves) | Purchasing function across activities |

The analytical habit: for **every** activity ask *does this add value the customer pays for, or is it support?* Activities that do neither are cost with no return — the candidates for outsourcing, automation, or removal.

## The margin view

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  C[Costs across all activities] --> M[Margin = price - costs]
  V[Value / differentiation the customer perceives] --> M
```

The chain's whole point is **margin**: every activity adds cost, and only some add value the customer perceives. Two strategies flow from this:

- **Cost leadership** — optimize the chain so your total cost beats rivals at the same price. Cost is decided across the *whole chain*, not one activity: a company with a slightly more expensive factory but dramatically cheaper logistics can still lead on cost.
- **Differentiation** — make specific activities (design, service, brand, the platform's developer experience) better than rivals so customers pay a premium. Differentiation lives in specific activities, not "being good overall."

The architecture translation: when you map value to systems, **cost-leadership estates are built to minimize operational cost** (consolidate, standardize, outsource the commodity, automate support); **differentiation estates invest heaviest in the differentiated activities' systems** (a best-in-class e-commerce platform, a proprietary analytics capability), even if the surrounding support systems stay generic.

## Five forces: what shapes the value you can keep

```mermaid
flowchart TB
  NE[New entrants] -->|threat| RA[Industry rivalry]
  BU[Buyer power] -->|pressure| RA
  SU[Supplier power] -->|pressure| RA
  SU[Substitutes] -->|threat| RA
  RA --> P[Profit pool in the industry]
```

Porter's companion idea: your ability to *keep* margin is set by the structure around the chain, not just your own activities. Strong supplier power (few suppliers, switching costs) squeezes your cost side; strong buyer power squeezes your price side; entrants and substitutes cap what you can charge. The five forces explain why two identical chains in different industries have very different margins — the airline chain is structurally thin, the chip chain structurally fat.

## Value chain vs supply chain vs business model

| Frame | Unit | Asks | Answers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Value chain** (Porter) | Activities | Which activities create value and margin? | Where to differentiate, outsource, invest |
| **Supply chain** (SCM) | Materials & flow | How does stuff flow plan→source→make→deliver? | How to run the flow with accuracy and speed |
| **Business model** | Economic logic | How is money made? | What's the revenue/cost engine and who pays |
| **Business process** (BPM) | Process steps | Who does what in what order? | How to model, automate, and govern work |

The value chain is the **strategy layer**: it says *which activities we must be best at*. The [supply chain](../supply-chain-management) and the [business processes](../business-process-modeling-and-management) are the *operation layers* that then implement those activities. When a value chain says "service is our differentiator," the estate responds with a service platform, not a cheaper factory system.

## Outsourcing and make-vs-buy, by activity

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  Act{Which activity?} --> Core{Core / differentiated?}
  Core -->|yes - strategic| Keep[Own and invest]
  Core -->|no - support or commodity| Out[Outsource / buy / automate]
  Core -->|margin check| M{Adds value customer pays for?}
  M -->|yes| Keep
  M -->|no| Out
```

The value chain turns make-vs-buy from a hunch into a checklist: an activity is a candidate for outsourcing when it is (a) **support rather than primary**, (b) **a commodity anyone can do** (IT support, payroll, logistics for many firms), or (c) **a support activity where an external specialist is better and cheaper**. It is a candidate for in-house investment when it is core to differentiation — because outsourcing a differentiator outsources the reason customers choose you.

| Activity | Typical treatment | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payroll, IT helpdesk | Outsource | Support, commodity, scale external specialists |
| Manufacturing (many brands) | Outsource (contract manufacturing) | Not the differentiator; capital-light wins |
| Customer experience / brand | Own | Directly differentiates |
| Core platform / data | Own | The proprietary advantage |

## The architectural questions

- **Where is the profit pool in your chain?** Map the estate's investment budget against the activities that earn margin — the mismatch is where technology spend is being wasted.
- **Which activities differentiate?** Those systems get the best-in-class treatment; everything else can be commodity, cloud, or off-the-shelf.
- **What do you actually outsource?** Every outsourced activity becomes an integration problem: data crosses a company boundary, so the [integration patterns](../integration-patterns) apply, and a contract (SLA, API, data share) replaces an internal handshake.
- **Is your support stack cheap?** Cost-leadership firms keep support systems lean and standardized; gold-plating support activities is the quiet margin leak.

## Tester checklist

- Map a real business to the nine activities and score each: primary vs support, core vs commodity, value-added vs pure cost — the map should identify at least one outsourcing candidate and one must-own activity.
- For an outsourcing candidate, enumerate the estate impact: which data crosses the boundary, what integration (patterns, SLAs, APIs) must be built to replace the internal handshake.
- Verify the differentiator is funded: is the *differentiated* activity's system budget larger than a *support* activity's, or has gold-plating crept into the commodity side?
- Price the chain: sum the activity costs and confirm the margin claim — an activity that costs more than the value it adds is a candidate to cut, automate, or outsource.
- Stress the five forces: pick one force (e.g., supplier power) and identify what the estate must build (multi-source tooling, switching options) to reduce that pressure.
