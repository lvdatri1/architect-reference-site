Wi-Fi mesh networking

Many access points, one seamless network: mesh topology, wired vs wireless backhaul, roaming and 802.11r/k/v, and when mesh beats (and loses to) wired APs.

Network EngineerEndpoint Device EngineerEnterprise ArchitectDeveloper#network#wifi#architecture

What this system is

A Wi-Fi mesh is a single logical network made of many access points (APs) that coordinate: one SSID, one shared config, and seamless roaming between them. The "mesh" word specifically means APs talk to each other — wired or wirelessly — to relay traffic and hand clients off. It's the fix for "big house / thick walls / one router in the corner."

Mesh topology: the tree

Rendering diagram…

A mesh is a tree rooted at the gateway. Each satellite connects to exactly one parent (the root or another satellite) and relays. The critical trade-off: every wireless hop halves (or more) your throughput — a satellite two hops away is roughly a third to a half the speed of the root AP. This is why "mesh is slow" usually means "someone put a satellite two wireless hops out instead of wiring one of them."

Backhaul: wired is the cheat code

BackhaulSpeed realityBest for
Wired / EthernetEvery AP runs at full line rate — no hop taxEnterprise, offices, anywhere you can pull cable
Dedicated radioA tri-band mesh uses a separate radio just for backhaulHome/small office: good compromise
Shared radio (dual-band)Backhaul steals airtime from clients on the same bandAvoid if you can; daisy-chaining two hops is worst

The rule: wire every AP you possibly can. Ethernet backhaul isn't "cheating" — it's the same architecture as enterprise Wi-Fi, and it removes the halving tax entirely. A mesh is only worth the wireless-hop tax where cabling is impossible.

Roaming: how a client moves between APs

Rendering diagram…

Three standards make roaming smooth:

  • 802.11r (FT) — fast BSS transition: pre-shared keys so handoff skips the full re-authentication round-trip (the difference between a "blip" and a dropped call).
  • 802.11k (RM) — radio resource measurement: APs give the client a list of nearby APs to scan, so it doesn't probe blindly.
  • 802.11v (BSS) — AP-guided roaming: the network tells a sticky client "move to AP 2" instead of waiting for it to decide.

One SSID everywhere is non-negotiable. Splitting "attic Wi-Fi" and "office Wi-Fi" into two SSIDs forces devices to reconnect and interrupt when moving. One SSID + the three standards above = a client walks through the house without dropping a video call.

The mistakes repeatedly remembered

  • Treating mesh as "more speed": adding satellites without wiring them adds coverage but can reduce per-client throughput once the wireless backhaul is the bottleneck.
  • Two SSIDs ("Upstairs" / "Downstairs") killing roaming: one network, one name, always.
  • Daisy-chaining three satellites and wondering why the far room is slow — backhaul hops compound.
  • Placing satellites adjacent instead of spaced across the coverage area, so the mesh keeps relaying between neighbors and never actually covers the dead zone.

Tester checklist

  • Roam while streaming a video call: confirm the call holds (802.11r/k/v active) and note the handoff gap.
  • Measure throughput at a satellite one hop away vs the root: expect roughly half — and use that number to decide whether wiring that AP is worth it.
  • Verify one-SSID behavior: move around the home and confirm the device stays on the same network name while switching APs.
  • Stress the backhaul: saturate a satellite's client with traffic and confirm the root AP isn't also stuck (if it is, backhaul is sharing airtime).
  • Check roaming stickiness: a client parked under one AP while a better AP is adjacent should get 802.11v guidance to move.
