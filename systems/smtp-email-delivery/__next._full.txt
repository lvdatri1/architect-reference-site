## What this system is

SMTP (Simple Mail Transfer Protocol) is the **store-and-forward** protocol that moves email between servers. It is 40+ years old, still goes over TCP/25 between mail servers, and it never, ever blocks waiting for a recipient to be online: mail is accepted, queued, and retried — that's the "store and forward" that makes it different from a chat protocol.

An email has two halves: the **envelope** (who to deliver to, used by SMTP: `MAIL FROM` and `RCPT TO`) and the **message** (headers + body, including `From:`/`To:` which are just text). The envelope is the contract between mail servers; the headers are what your mail client displays. Forgers abuse the gap between them, which is why SPF, DKIM, and DMARC exist.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  MU[Sender MUA - Outlook / Gmail app] -->|submit mail| SU[Submission server - port 587 STARTTLS]
  SU -->|queue| MT[Sender MTA - queue + retry]
  MT -->|MX lookup, SMTP on port 25| MX[Recipient MX - accepted by MX]
  MX -->|deliver to mailbox| LD[LDA to mailbox - IMAP / POP serves it]
```

## The path an email takes

```mermaid
sequenceDiagram
  participant U as Sender (MUA)
  participant S as Submission :587
  participant M as Sender MTA (queue)
  participant X as Recipient MX :25
  participant L as Mailbox (IMAP)

  U->>S: SMTP AUTH + STARTTLS, MAIL FROM / RCPT TO / DATA
  S->>M: accept, add Received + headers
  M->>X: DNS MX lookup → connect :25
  M->>X: EHLO, MAIL FROM, RCPT TO, DATA
  X->>M: 250 OK, queue it
  X->>L: deliver to user's mailbox
  L->>U: client pulls via IMAP
```

Every **MTA→MTA** hop re-writes the envelope path in `Received:` headers — that chain is your forensic trail (the "Received: by ... from ..." block you can read in any spam).

## The SMTP conversation

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Client] -->|EHLO example.com| B[Server]
  B -->|250-hello| A
  A -->|MAIL FROM: bob@example.com| B
  B -->|250 2.1.0 Ok| A
  A -->|RCPT TO: alice@corp.net| B
  B -->|250 2.1.5 Ok| A
  A -->|DATA| B
  B -->|354 End data with CRLF-CRLF| A
  A -->|headers + body| B
  B -->|250 2.0.0 Ok queued| A
  A -->|QUIT| B
```

Key facts to internalize:

- **Ports**: `:25` is MTA→MTA relay; `:587` is client submission (with STARTTLS); `:465` is implicit-TLS submission (legacy). Port 25 has no encryption by default — that's why submission uses 587.
- **MAIL FROM ≠ From:**. One is the bounce/return path (envelope), the other is what your client shows. Spam scores both.
- **Retry, don't fail**: a temporary failure (`4xx`) triggers queue + retry with exponential backoff (minutes → hours, typically up to 2 days). A permanent failure (`5xx`, e.g. unknown user) means **bounce** (a delivery-status notification sent back to the envelope `MAIL FROM`).
- **Received headers** get prepended at each hop; the *lowest* `Received:` on your received email is where it entered your org.

## MX records: finding where to send

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  S[Your MTA] -->|A? / AAAA? mx1.corp.net| D[DNS]
  D -->|priority 10 → mx1, 20 → mx2| S
  S -->|try mx1 :25, fail → retry queue| S
  S -->|connect mx1| E[Recipient MX]
```

The MX record lists mail servers **by preference number** (lower = higher priority). Mail is tried on the lowest-preference MX; if that fails, the next. MX records for the same domain may point to different networks — that's your redundancy. An SMTP server is not required to accept mail for a domain just because it has an A record; only MX (or an implicit A if no MX) says "this host receives mail for me."

## SPF, DKIM, DMARC — the email armor

Email has no built-in proof of who sent it, so the industry layered DNS records on top:

| Mechanism | What it verifies | DNS record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SPF** | That the sending IP is *authorized* by the domain's policy | `TXT v=spf1 ip4:... include:_spf.google.com ~all` |
| **DKIM** | That the message body + selected headers were *signed* by a key the domain publishes | `TXT` at `selector._domainkey.example.com` with a public key |
| **DMARC** | A policy telling receivers what to do when SPF/DKIM fail | `TXT _dmarc.example.com v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:...` |

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Sender signs with private DKIM key] --> B[Message: DKIM-Signature header]
  B --> C[Recipient queries domain key via DNS]
  C --> D[Verify signature + SPF source + DMARC policy]
  D -->|align From header with envelope domain| E[Quarantine or reject if unaligned]
```

DMARC is the piece that actually changes behavior: SPF/DKIM prove *something* about the sender, but DMARC's `p=reject` is what makes receivers drop messages where the visible `From:` domain doesn't align with the authenticated sender. That's also why DMARC is the fence for **domain spoofing** — and why you should check `_dmarc` before you trust a lookalike domain.

## Bounces, and why you never configure bounce loops

- **5xx** = permanent → bounce immediately (mailbox full is actually 4xx temporary). 
- **4xx** = temporary → queue and retry with backoff.
- If a server's `MAIL FROM` is empty (`<>`), it's a **delivery-status notification** (a bounce); mail servers *must not* bounce a bounce, or you get a loop.

## Tester checklist

- Inspect the full header chain of a suspicious email (`Show original` in Gmail): are the `Received:` hops plausible, and does DMARC pass?
- Send to a test mailbox and confirm the `Received:` `from` header records the real sending IP — then check that IP against SPF.
- Force a `4xx` (e.g., pause the MX) and verify your MTA queues and retries with growing delays — a flapping MX must not drop mail.
- Verify TLS in transit: submission to your provider uses STARTTLS (see the `220` banner), and MTA-to-MTA supports it where the peer does.
- Test DMARC alignment: send with `From:` different from the envelope domain and confirm the policy (`p=none` should be your *monitor first* mode).
