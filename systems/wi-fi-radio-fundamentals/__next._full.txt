## What this system is

Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11) is a **shared radio medium**, which is the whole story: unlike a switch, only one client can transmit on a channel at a time, they all hear each other, and the medium is half-duplex. Every Wi-Fi problem — slow LAN nights, dropped video calls, "the microwave kills my connection" — is a symptom of radio physics, not (only) bad firmware.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  CL[Client device - Wi-Fi radio] -->|radio frame 2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz| AP[Access point - SSID on a channel]
  AP -->|802.1Q VLAN| SW[Switch / VLAN]
  SW -->|routed| RT[Router / gateway]
  RT -->|WAN| IN[Internet]
```

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  C[Client] -->|half-duplex radio frames| AP[Access point - one channel, shared]
  AP --> SW[Switch - full duplex] --> RT[Router] --> IN[Internet]
```

## Bands, channels, and overlap

| Band | Typical channels | Reality | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2.4 GHz | 1-13, but really 1/6/11 non-overlapping | 3 usable channels; everyone+microwave+Bluetooth; long range, low throughput | Range, IoT, legacy |
| 5 GHz | 36+ (UNII-1/2, DFS) | Many non-overlapping channels, higher data rates | Modern clients, less crowded |
| 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E/7) | Lots, clean | Exclusive to 6E+ gear, no legacy interference | The empty highway |

Adjacent channels in the **same band interfere** because of wavelength skirts — 2.4 GHz's 20 MHz channel spacing means channels 1, 6, and 11 are the only truly non-overlapping set. A neighbor AP on channel 2 chews your channel 1. This is why enterprise surveys put APs on overlapping channels: neighbors on **different channels** to minimize their mutual interference.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[AP on ch 1] -->|20 MHz| CH1[ch 1]
  B[AP on ch 6] -->|20 MHz| CH6[ch 6]
  C[AP on ch 11] -->|20 MHz| CH11[ch 11]
  CH1 --> N1[neighbors on 2,3,4 interfere]
  CH6 --> N2[neighbors on 5,7 interfere]
  CH11 --> N3[neighbors on 10,12 interfere]
```

## CSMA/CA: why Wi-Fi is half-duplex and "polite"

```mermaid
sequenceDiagram
  participant A as Client A
  participant W as Wireless medium (shared)
  participant B as Client B

  A->>W: listen (carrier sense) - clear?
  A->>W: send frame → W
  A->>W: wait for ACK
  B->>W: listen - medium busy, back off
  B->>W: after DIFS + random backoff, try again
  W->>B: ACK received - successful
```

Ethernet is cabled and collision-free per port; Wi-Fi must **listen before talking** (carrier sense) and **collision-avoid** (CSMA/CA) with random backoff, then wait for an **ACK**. Only one transmission per channel at a time means: more clients = more waiting = the "it's slow at dinner" effect, all else equal.

## The signals: RSSI, SNR, and how you tell "bad" from "bad area"

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  AP -->|signal + noise| C[Client]
  C --> M[Measure RSSI and noise]
  M --> S[SNR = signal - noise]
  S --> V{Decision}
  V -->|low RSSI -150 dBm| D[Move closer / more APs]
  V -->|high RSSI but low SNR| E[Interference - change channel / band]
  V -->|good SNR -25 dB| F[Data rate ramps up]
```

- **RSSI** (dBm): how strong the received signal is. -40 dBm = excellent, -85 dBm = borderline, less = roaming time.
- **SNR** (dB): signal strength *relative to noise* — this is what actually determines usable data rate, not raw RSSI. High RSSI next to a loud noise source is still bad Wi-Fi.
- **Data rate** falls off the cliff as SNR drops: one bad neighbor on your channel (or a per-user 40 MHz channel that needs more headroom) drops you from 400 Mb/s to 12.

## SSID, bands, and security (WPA for short)

- **One SSID, many bands**: modern APs broadcast one SSID across 2.4/5/6 GHz; clients band-steer where possible. SSID is a name, *not* a security boundary — there's no isolation by name unless you configure it.
- **WPA2** (AES-CCMP) is the floor. **WPA3** (SAE + forward secrecy, modern handshake, easy with WPA2-compatible transition mode) is the default for anything new. WPA2-PSK shared passwords are only as strong as the password and age.
- **Enterprise (802.1X/EAP)**: each user gets a certificate or credential exchange — the default for corporate networks precisely because shared-PSK weakens the moment someone leaks or loses a device.
- **Hidden SSIDs do nothing for security** (clients boilerplate-broadcast for the hidden name) and slow roaming: leave broadcast on.

## The mistake repeatedly remembered

- Two APs on the same channel stepping on each other = "AP 2 has no internet" when it's really interference.
- Turning the 2.4 GHz radio off for "speed" and then wondering why IoT devices can't join.
- Buying a "WPA" router for a post-2019 device, then warning staff about "the easy Wi-Fi."
- Placing the AP behind a metal shelf, in a cupboard, or on the floor, and treating the poor result as a config bug.

## Tester checklist

- Walk the floor with a client tool (or `wi-fi inspector`-style survey): confirm RSSI stays better than -70 dBm in usable spaces.
- Check for rogue/duplicate SSIDs or an AP on an overlapping channel (wigle/netspot-style scan) — you may be your own interference.
- Force a client to the 5 GHz band and confirm band-steering moves it off 2.4 when capable.
- Verify WPA3/enterprise: the signing happens at the AP; confirm WPA2-only devices still join (transition mode) while WPA3 clients get SAE.
- Watch `ACK` behavior under load: a busy-open client list (many slow ACKs) is a capacity, not speed, problem.
