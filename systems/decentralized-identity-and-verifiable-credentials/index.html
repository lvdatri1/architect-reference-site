Decentralized Identity and Verifiable Credentials

Self-sovereign identity: Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs), verifiable credentials, and the issuer-holder-verifier triangle replacing centralized account databases.

Security ArchitectSolution ArchitectDeveloperEnterprise ArchitectTester#identity#decentralization#verifiable-credentials#ssi#cryptography#blockchain

What this system is

Decentralized identity (self-sovereign identity, SSI) moves identity out of the silo of an account database. Instead of every organization issuing you an account you must log into, you hold verifiable credentials (VCs) — digitally signed claims like "is employee of Acme", "over 18", "licensed to drive" — in your own wallet, and present them to anyone who needs to verify.

The two core building blocks:

  • Decentralized Identifier (DID): a globally unique, resolvable identifier with no central registrar. It looks like did:example:abc123... and resolves (via a DID Document) to the public keys used to sign.
  • Verifiable Credential: a JSON document signed by an issuer, given to a holder, and presentable to a verifier. The cryptographic signatures (and often a blockchain anchor) make the claim tamper-evident and reputable without a phone call back to headquarters.

The problem it solves

Traditional identityDecentralized identity
Every service runs its own account table and password reset flowOne wallet holds credentials; services just verify presentations
The credential is the account — sign in to prove itThe credential is a signed claim — the signature proves it
Services collect and correlate your data because they must store identitySelective disclosure: present only the fields you need ("is over 18", not my birthday)
Anyone with the master record can impersonate youOnly the wallet with the private key can present the credential
Identity is bounded by the cloud account of one vendorDIDs are portable across issuers, wallets, and verifiers

The design point is delegation of trust: the verifier does not need to trust the holder (they might be malicious) or talk to the issuer (who might be offline). It only needs the issuer's public key, which is published once and can be cryptographically verified, possibly anchored in a blockchain so the key rotation itself has an auditable history.

Key concepts

  • DID method: each did:myMethod: prefix defines how the DID is created, resolved, and updated (e.g., registered on a blockchain, or propagated via a peer's own servers). The method is the trust anchor's protocol.
  • DID document: the resource a DID resolves to — public keys, service endpoints, and authentication material. Rotating a key is updating the document, not re-issuing every credential.
  • ZKP / selective disclosure: cryptographically prove "this statement about my claim is true" without revealing the underlying data — e.g., prove an age range, not a birth date.
  • Revocation: revoking a credential means the issuer updates a status registry (often an on-chain accumulator, a message list, or a status list through the conditional-access-style policy engine). A verifier must check revocation, or a revoked ("terminated") credential still looks valid.

When decentralized identity fits

  • Cross-organization onboarding — hire once, present the same credential everywhere (see Joiner–Mover–Leaver for the JML twist).
  • Regulated privacy — health, finance, and education must minimize data sharing; selective disclosure reduces the surface.
  • Supply chain provenance — prove a part was certified at each step without revealing the whole contract.
  • Wallets as an integration surface — abstract away the account sprawl of SCIM provisioning by having users carry their own verified attributes.

The hard parts are credential trust (which issuers do you accept?) and revocation speed. If the ecosystem cannot decide who is a reputable issuer, or if revocation is a day-late cached list, you inherit the very risk you removed.

Tester checklist

  • Verify a VC's signature: tamper with one field and confirm presentation fails by the issuer's published key.
  • Test selective disclosure: request "over 18" and confirm the birth date is never transmitted.
  • Check revocation: revoke a credential and confirm a verifier that consults the status registry rejects it promptly.
  • Validate key rotation: rotate the issuer's DID key and confirm existing credentials still verify against the rotated key.
  • Test the wallet boundary: the private key must never leave the wallet, and a UI must be able to request presentation without reading the key.
  • Exercise the replay case: can a presented credential be replayed by a different device, and is that controlled by binding presentation to an audience/nonce?
  • Confirm interoperability: the DID method, VC format, and status mechanism match what both issuer and verifier announce — no private extensions.
  • Check the failure path: an offline registry or unreachable DID must fail closed (deny), not degrade to "trust everyone".
